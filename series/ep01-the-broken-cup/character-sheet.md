## MAYA — hero

**Locked sheet:** [`/characters/hero.md`](../../characters/hero.md)

**Locked visual-prompt paragraph — copy verbatim into every image generation featuring Maya:**

> A 7-year-old girl with curly dark brown hair in two short puffs, warm brown skin, round face with a gap-tooth smile and light freckles across her nose. Wearing a yellow t-shirt and teal overalls, one shoe always slightly untied. Flat, rounded cutout animation style, soft shapes, warm daytime lighting.

Change the pose and the action only. Never the hair, the outfit, the freckles, or the untied shoe — not even across the morning-to-afternoon jump between Scene 1 and Scene 6.

### What this episode asks of Maya

**The nose scrunch — once, and only once.** Her locked tell for an easy-wrong choice fires inside the Scene 2 pause, in the last beat before her first false word, immediately ahead of **"It just fell."** Hold one full beat after it, no music under it. **Do not use the tell anywhere else in the episode.** This is also the Shorts moment (backlog `shorts_hook`: the moment right before Maya decides what to say), so it has to be clean and it has to be the only one.

**Two later beats deliberately echo that pause without the tell.** Play the same shape — mouth open, eyes up to the shelf, nothing comes out — and keep the nose still, so the one scrunch stays the only one:
- Scene 3, before **"Could you fix it? Your cup."**
- Scene 4, before **"The shelf's fine. It didn't just fall."** — the pause she finally speaks through.

**She tries the wrong thing first, and she means it.** The lie has a real motive on screen: she was reaching for the cup to bring him his tea, "like a real job, a big one," and dropping it means she isn't the careful helper any more. She props the lie up ("Maybe the wind did it") rather than abandoning it. Nobody catches her — the lie fails because of what it costs her, not because she gets found out. Never play her as foolish, and never let the animation mock her for it.

**Shoulder line carries the whole middle of the episode.** They come up with the breath before the lie in Scene 2 and stay up. They come partway down on the sentence itself — like setting something heavy down that she knows she'll pick back up. They do not come all the way down until the relief breath in Scene 4. Treat this as her posture continuity spine across Scenes 2–4.

**Smiles are rationed.** She does not smile once between frame one and Scene 5. Her first smile is at Gramps Lou's "Took me till dinner." The locked gap-tooth grin lands in the Closing, on the tap of the mended cup.

**She's competent, and the lie is what makes her look otherwise.** Bolt-sorting and line-marking are the two jobs she loves at this stand. In Scene 3 she puts small bolts in the big jar and never looks down, hands him a wrench instead of a band, and says "No thanks" to the pencil — all things she would never do. In Scene 5, after the truth, she lines five pieces up biggest to smallest with nobody asking. Animate the Scene 5 competence as plainly as the Scene 3 fumbles; the contrast is the cost of the lie.

**The one flat-hand gesture, used twice.** She opens her hand flat and tips it over — first in Scene 4 to show how the cup went while she tells the truth, then in Scene 5 to work out which way the handle flew. Same gesture, second time as a tool. Match the two animations exactly.

**The only fourth-wall look in the episode is hers, in the Closing, and it is an invitation.** Warm and easy, the way you'd look at a friend across the street — not a lecture, not a lesson, not a straight-to-camera moral. Nobody else ever looks at camera, including Gramps Lou, who stays sanding at the frame edge through the whole beat.

**She names the skill, and it stays hers.** Scene 6: *"Telling the truth was hard for one minute, but the lie was hard all day."* One plain sentence, spoken by the kid. It is not repeated, explained, improved, or echoed by the adult.

**Continuity marks on the locked look (temporary, never a redesign):**
- Frame one: one curved piece of the cup has rocked to a stop against her untied shoe. The untied shoe is locked, not a prop — it is there anyway.
- Scene 3: the warm peach leaves a wet ring on her overalls. This is a mark on the locked outfit, not a costume change; carry it forward consistently once it appears. The script never says it dries — hold it or let it fade, but do not swap the overalls.
- Scene 5: a stripe of dust across one cheek when she backs out from under the bench, scrubbed off with her sleeve in the same shot. Clean from that shot on.
- Scene 5: she is flat on her stomach under the bench in a static wide — two legs sticking out, one shoe untied. No cut-in, no flashlight.

## GRAMPS LOU — adult

**Locked sheet:** [`/characters/adult.md`](../../characters/adult.md)

**Locked visual-prompt paragraph — copy verbatim into every image generation featuring Gramps Lou:**

> An older man with a gray beard and a flat cap, deep brown skin, wearing a denim work apron with rolled-up plaid shirt sleeves, usually holding a tool or sorting small parts. Flat, rounded cutout animation style, soft shapes, warm daytime lighting.

Change the pose and the action only. The cap, the beard, and the apron never change.

### What this episode asks of Gramps Lou

**A visible physical task in every scene he is in — named here, scene by scene.** Per his locked sheet he is never just standing and talking:

| Scene | His visible task |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Sawing, out of frame** — shh-shh, shh-shh, and it does not stop. The saw is why he never hears the cup break. He is heard, not seen, in this scene; the task still has to be audible and continuous, and it stops only after "You okay out there?" |
| 2 | **Carrying and setting down a sanded board**, sanding block in hand. Then crouching to turn one curved piece over in his fingers, picking the pieces one at a time into his palm, tipping them into the cardboard box, setting the box up in the dust ring, and picking the board back up to go back to work. |
| 3 | **Gluing a cracked chair rung** — white glue brushed in slow and careful. Then stretching the fat red band around the chair leg and snapping it tight, standing the chair in the sun, getting a peach out of the cooler and wiping it on his apron, **winding a cord around his elbow**, then pulling a strip off the scrap pile, sighting down it, marking the line himself when she says no, two saw strokes, blowing the sawdust off. |
| 4 | **Standing the cut strip against the shelf and making two pencil dots**, then picking up the drill. When she says "Wait!" he sets the drill down on the bench, bit up. Through her silence he **wipes glue off his fingers with a rag, one finger at a time, eyes on his own hands** — busy, not waiting her out. Then he sets the folded rag down, and later **rubs the two pencil dots off the shelf with his thumb**. |
| 5 | **One hand steadying the crate** while she climbs. Opening the third drawer down for the small tube of glue, handing her a dry paintbrush, **holding two pieces together with his big thumbs** while she does the next one, cutting a red rubber band and looping it twice around the cup, standing it in the sun beside the drying chair. |
| 6 | **Sanding the cut strip smooth**, long even strokes — the strip he no longer needs. Then taking the flat carpenter pencil from behind his ear and holding it out to her, blunt end first. |
| Closing | **Still sanding**, at the far edge of the frame. Not looking at camera, not talking, not part of the "your turn" beat. |

**He asks, he never lectures, and he never monologues.** His longest line in the episode is eleven words. Every line he has is a question, a short answer, or a one-line instruction. If a performance or a pickup ever stretches one of his lines into an explanation, it is wrong.

**He never catches her, and he never sets a trap.** No detective face, no long stare, no raised eyebrow, no meaningful pause after "Maybe." When she refuses the pencil in Scene 3 he simply marks the line himself, with no comment. In Scene 4 he gives her a genuine way out — *"Or you want to keep sorting. Both are fine with me."* Keep every frame of his wide and easy. The story turns on Maya's choice, not on adult pressure.

**He is allowed to be wrong.** Believing her, he concludes the shelf must be sloping and starts building a lip for a shelf that was fine all along. He is not omniscient; play the shelf idea as sincere problem-solving, not as a test.

**He owns the same mistake.** Scene 5: *"Me." / "Dropped it in the sink." / "Took me till dinner."* Flat and unembarrassed, no wink, not a teaching moment — he is just answering her question while his thumbs hold a cup shut.

**He does not resolve the feeling for her.** *"About the cup? A little." / "Not at you."* He is honestly a little mad, and says so in two short lines. Never soften this into "it's okay" — the episode needs the small true version.

**He does not touch the skill line.** One nod after it, sanding continues, and nothing added.

## No one-off character in this episode

**There is no one-off side character in EP01, and that is a decision, not an omission.**

The backlog row's `conflict` field reads, exactly: *"The cup is broken on the floor and Maya is the only one who was in the room."* That is the whole engine of the episode. Adding any third figure — a kid at the stand, a customer, a neighbor passing — breaks it in one of two ways:

- If they **saw** it, the lie is pointless and the story becomes about getting caught, not about choosing.
- If they **didn't see** it, they still have to be handled on screen every time Maya is alone with what she did, and every silent beat in Scenes 2–4 has to route around them.

The episode's silence belongs to Maya. Nobody else can be in it.

**On BIBLE.md's one-off-character example.** BIBLE.md used to cite "Sam in the pilot," which was stale: per `trackers/episode-backlog.csv`, Sam belongs to **ep05, "Sam's Crayon"** — a classroom episode. Sam does not appear in EP01 in any form, and could not: the backlog row's conflict requires Maya to be the only one in the room. BIBLE.md has since been corrected to cite ep05. The one-off-characters-are-allowed rule itself was always fine; only the example pointed at the wrong episode.
